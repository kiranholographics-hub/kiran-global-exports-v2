Buyers researching towel suppliers run into both terms within the first few search results, often used as if they mean the same thing. They don't, and the difference matters before you write a brief.

## White label: the product stays the same, the label changes

A white label product is a standard, existing item — a supplier's regular stock specification — sold under different brands to different buyers. The towel itself, its GSM, construction, colour range and sizing are fixed by the manufacturer, not the buyer. What changes from customer to customer is the label sewn in and the packaging it ships in. Multiple retailers can be selling the identical towel under different names without knowing it.

## Private label: the specification is built around the buyer

A private label product starts from the buyer's brief rather than a supplier's existing catalogue. GSM, size, colour, border design, weave and construction are built to specification, and the woven label, hang tags and packaging are the buyer's own. Two private label buyers ordering from the same mill can end up with genuinely different products, because the specification itself was theirs to set, not just the branding on top of it.

## What actually stays the same in either case

Regardless of which route a buyer takes, the underlying production process doesn't change — raw material sourcing, weaving, dyeing, quality control and shipping documentation are handled the same way. What changes is how much of the product's actual specification the buyer controlled versus inherited from a standard offering.

## Why this distinction changes what you ask for

If you want a fast, low-friction way to get a branded product to market and a supplier's existing quality bar is genuinely good enough for your customer, white label is the quicker and usually cheaper path — there's no development cycle because the specification already exists. If your brand's positioning depends on a specific weight, hand-feel, colourway or construction that a standard catalogue doesn't offer, that's a private label brief, and it should be treated as one from the first conversation — not requested as "private label" and then quietly built from an existing stock item with a new tag sewn in.

## The question worth asking before you request either

Before naming either term to a supplier, it's worth asking yourself what you actually need protected: is it the brand on the label, or the specification underneath it? A private label brief that only ever specifies "put our label on your best towel" is functionally a white label order with private label pricing — and a supplier that doesn't ask what you actually want to control on the product itself is a sign to ask more questions, not fewer.
