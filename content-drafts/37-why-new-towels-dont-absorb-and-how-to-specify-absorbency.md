A new towel often takes up water more slowly than the same towel after a few washes. The usual causes are on the surface of the cotton: a finish applied for softness, leftover preparation chemicals, or natural wax that was not fully removed. Later, laundry habits can cause the same problem again. None of this means the towel is faulty, but it does mean that "absorbent" on a quotation is only worth something if it says how absorbency was measured and on what.

This guide explains what lowers absorbency, which tests are used to measure it, and how to write an absorbency line that a mill and an inspector will read the same way. It is general guidance. It does not describe the finishing used on any towel we make, and it does not set a pass mark.

## What lowers absorbency

| Cause | Where it happens | What the sources say | What you can do |
|---|---|---|---|
| Natural wax left on the cotton | Preparation, before dyeing | Raw cotton carries a wax that repels water. Scouring, a hot alkaline wash, breaks it down, and the sources agree that most of it must go for the cloth to take up water well | Ask whether the towel is scoured and tested for absorbency, not only whether it is soft |
| A softening finish | Finishing | Softeners improve the hand but can lower the rate at which water is taken up. Plain silicone softeners are described as water-repellent, and finish makers sell versions designed to keep water uptake | Ask which type of finish is used and whether absorbency is tested after it is applied |
| Excess dye, loose fibre and finish residue | Production | A first wash removes much of this. Sources disagree on how much it matters: some call it essential, others say the effect is slight | Ask for absorbency results after washing as well as as-received |
| Fabric softener in the laundry | After delivery | Repeated use is widely advised against on towels, because it can leave a film on the cotton | Tell the laundry not to use it on towels |
| Too much detergent | After delivery | Residue that is not rinsed out can build up and slow water uptake | Check the dosing and the rinse |
| The construction | Design | Yarn and weave change absorbency. Our guide to towel constructions covers zero twist, ringspun and waffle, and our GSM guide explains why weight alone is not a measure | Specify the construction, and test it |

The first three and the last are set before the towel leaves the mill. The two laundry causes start afterwards, which is why a towel can absorb well on the day it is tested and badly in service. A spec can control the first group, and your laundry procedure the second.

## How absorbency is tested

Absorbency is not one measurement. The two methods you will most often see answer different questions.

| Method | What it does | What it does not tell you |
|---|---|---|
| AATCC TM79, the drop test | Places a drop of water on the fabric and records how long it takes for the drop to be taken up. A shorter time means faster wetting | How much water the towel holds, how far it wicks, or how fast it dries |
| ASTM D4772, the water flow test for terry | Measures how rapidly a terry fabric absorbs and retains water from a surface. Its scope names bath towels, hand towels, washcloths and bathrobes | Anything about non-terry fabrics such as huck, which it excludes. ASTM also says that precision between laboratories is known to be poor, so it advises caution when the result is used to accept or reject a shipment |

A test report that says only "absorbency: pass" does not tell you which of these was used, so the result cannot be compared with another report. Our guide to reading a towel test report explains what a report must show. Check which edition of a standard is cited, as these standards are revised.

## Worked example: why the wash matters

The figures below are hypothetical and show how to read a drop test. They are not our results and not a limit.

| Sample | Drop test time |
|---|---|
| As received | 12 seconds |
| After 3 washes | 3 seconds |

The same towel is four times slower as received (12 ÷ 3 = 4). A buyer who tested only the as-received towel would reject a towel that behaves normally in service, and a buyer who tested only the washed towel would miss that a guest drying off with a new towel feels the slower one. Neither number is wrong. They answer different questions, so a spec should say which one it means.

## How to write an absorbency line

A usable line names the method, the state of the towel and the reference. For example:

- Method and edition, such as ASTM D4772 for terry, or AATCC TM79 where a drop test is wanted.
- The state of the towel: as received, or after a stated number of washes using a stated procedure.
- The reference: not worse than the approved sample, tested the same way.
- Who tests it and where. Results from different laboratories may not agree.

We do not give a pass mark here, because the acceptable figure depends on the product and the method. Set it with your supplier from an approved sample. Our guide to reading a towel spec sheet covers how the other lines should be written.

## Checking a sample yourself

A quick check does not replace a laboratory test, but it is useful when you compare samples:

- Test the sample as received, then wash it the way your laundry washes towels and test it again.
- Use the same room, the same water and the same method for every sample, including the approved one.
- Compare like with like. A waffle towel and a terry towel are not comparable on a drop test.
- Record the result with a photograph, and keep the sample.

Our guide to reading a pre-shipment sample covers what else to check on the sample.

## What this guide does not cover

It does not give a pass mark for any method, because the sources used for it do not set one for your product. It does not cover how fast a towel dries, which is a separate property. It does not say which finish any towel we make has, since that is confirmed on quotation against your brief. Some of the points about finishes come from the technical literature of finish makers, who have an interest in their own products, so treat them as explanations, not as test results. Sources disagree on how much the first wash matters, so test your own sample.

## What to ask a supplier

- Is the towel scoured, and is absorbency tested after finishing?
- What type of softening finish is used, and does it affect absorbency?
- Which method and edition is the absorbency result from, and was it tested as received or after washing?
- How many washes, and by which procedure?
- Can I have a sample to test as received and after washing?
