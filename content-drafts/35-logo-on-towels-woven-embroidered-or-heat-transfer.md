There are three common ways to put a logo on a towel, and they are not interchangeable. A woven logo, whether a woven label or a design woven into the cloth, is the most durable and the most work to set up. Embroidery sits in the middle on cost and on durability. Heat-transfer is the cheapest and quickest to sample, and the first to show wear after repeated commercial washing. Which one suits your order depends on how long the towel will stay in service, how many you are ordering, and how much of the cost you want to pay up front.

This guide explains what each method is, what to check before you choose, and what to send a manufacturer so the sample shows the logo you actually want. It is general guidance; the methods we can offer and the quantities that suit them are confirmed on quotation, against your brief.

## The three methods

| Method | How the logo is made | Durability in laundry | What to watch |
|---|---|---|---|
| Woven (label or jacquard) | A label is woven separately on a small loom and attached to the towel. In jacquard, the design is woven into the cloth itself, usually in a border or band | The most durable of the three | The set-up is the most work, so it suits a longer run. A woven label is made separately and then attached, so check how it is attached |
| Embroidered | Thread is stitched through the towel from a digitised file | In the middle | Dense designs can pucker the pile around them. Terry is looped, so the stitching needs a water-soluble topping to keep it from sinking into the loops. Ask whether the factory uses one |
| Heat-transfer | A printed design is bonded to the surface with heat | The first to show wear after repeated commercial washing | Cheapest and fastest to sample, so it suits a short run or a first trial, not a towel expected to stay in service for years |

Where branding has to survive sun and chlorine, such as on pool and beach towels, woven labels and jacquard borders tend to hold up best, and heat-transfer is usually the first element to crack.

## How set-up cost spreads over the run

Some methods charge to prepare the logo before a single towel is made, and others charge more per piece. The break-even point depends on the quantity.

The figures here are hypothetical, to show the arithmetic. They are not our prices. Suppose one method has a set-up charge of 300 and a cost of 0.10 per piece, and another has no set-up charge and costs 0.25 per piece.

- For 1,000 pieces, the first costs 300 + 1,000 × 0.10 = 400, and the second costs 1,000 × 0.25 = 250.
- For 5,000 pieces, the first costs 300 + 5,000 × 0.10 = 800, and the second costs 5,000 × 0.25 = 1,250.
- The two cost the same at 300 ÷ (0.25 − 0.10) = 2,000 pieces. Below that quantity the second is cheaper, and above it the first is.

So a method that looks dear on a small order can be the cheaper one on a large order, and the reverse. Ask for the set-up charge and the per-piece cost separately, and compare on your own quantity. If you reorder, the set-up usually does not repeat, but confirm that with the manufacturer.

## What to decide before you ask for a quote

- **Where the towel will be used.** A hotel towel that is washed hot and often needs a more durable method than a promotional towel that is used a few times.
- **How long it should last.** If the logo should outlive the towel, woven or embroidered; if the programme is a short promotion, heat-transfer may be enough.
- **The quantity.** See the arithmetic above.
- **The size and detail of the logo.** Fine detail and small text are hard in any woven or stitched method, and a simplified version of the logo often works better.
- **The colours.** A woven or stitched logo uses thread or yarn colours, so give a reference for each, and approve them on the sample. Our guide to lab dips explains why a physical reference matters.
- **The position.** A border, a band or a corner. Say which, and the distance from the edge.

## What to send

- The logo as a vector file, such as AI, EPS or PDF, which is normally preferred over a photograph or a low-resolution image. For embroidery, the factory turns it into a stitch file.
- The size of the logo on the towel and its position.
- The colour of each element, with a physical or Pantone reference.
- The towel it goes on, with size and weight.
- The quantity, and whether you will reorder.
- Where and how the towel will be washed.

## Checking the sample

Approve the logo on a physical sample, not on a drawing. Look at the edges, the colours against your reference and how the logo sits on the towel. Then wash it the way your laundry washes towels, for several cycles, and look again: loose threads, a lifting edge, cracking, fading or colour running onto the towel. Our guide to reading a pre-shipment sample covers what else to check on the sample, and our guide to reading a towel test report explains how colourfastness is measured.

## What this guide does not cover

It does not give prices, minimum quantities or lead times for any method, which depend on the design and are confirmed on quotation. It does not compare methods through a laundry test, so the durability points above are general and should be confirmed on your own sample.

## What to ask a supplier

- Which method do you recommend for my towel, quantity and laundry, and why?
- What is the set-up charge, and what is the cost per piece, separately?
- How is a woven label attached?
- For embroidery, do you use a topping on the terry, and how dense is the design?
- Can I have a physical sample of the logo before the order, and can I wash it first?
