A cotton name on a quotation, such as "Egyptian", "Pima", "Supima" or "Indian", is a claim about the fibre, not a measurement of the towel. It can matter, because the cotton sets the ceiling on how fine, strong and smooth the yarn can be. But the name alone does not tell you which variety was used, how long the fibres are, how much of the towel is made from it, or whether anyone has checked. This guide explains what the names mean, what staple length changes in a towel, and what to ask for so a cotton claim in a quote can be checked.

It is general guidance for reading quotations. It does not rank cottons, and it does not describe the cotton in any towel we sell. That is confirmed on quotation, against your brief.

## What staple length is, and why it matters

Staple length is the length of the cotton fibres. Longer fibres can be spun into finer, stronger and smoother yarn, with fewer fibre ends sticking out. In a towel that tends to mean a smoother hand, less lint and loops that pull out less easily. Our guide to towel yarn explains how the cotton interacts with carding, combing and spinning, and why two towels of the same weight can still feel different.

Cotton is grouped by staple length into short, medium, long and extra-long, and the group boundaries are not the same in every scheme. As a rough guide, extra-long staple (ELS) is generally taken to mean fibres of about 33 to 35 mm or longer. Treat any single figure in a quote as the supplier's own, and ask which scheme it comes from.

Longer cotton is also scarcer and dearer. A high-volume hotel programme may not need extra-long fibre, and a premium retail range may. The right choice depends on the product, not on the name.

## What the common names mean

| Name | What it refers to | Is the name protected? | What to ask |
|---|---|---|---|
| Egyptian cotton | Cotton grown in Egypt, including long and extra-long staple types | The Egyptian Cotton logo is a registered trademark. Its licensing is run by the Cotton Egypt Association, and only products made from 100 per cent Egyptian cotton may carry it | Is the towel licensed to use the logo, under what licence, and which cotton variety is it |
| Pima | A type of long and extra-long staple cotton, grown in several countries including the United States and Peru | "Pima" is a general term, so the name alone does not tell you the country | Which country, which variety |
| Supima | A trademarked name for American-grown Pima cotton, promoted by an association of US growers | Yes, the name is a trademark and is licensed | Is the supplier licensed to use it, and for which product |
| Indian cotton | Cotton grown in India, which produces a wide range of staple lengths, including some extra-long staple types such as Suvin from Tamil Nadu | No | Which variety and staple length, if it matters to you |
| "Turkish" towel | May describe where the towel was made or its style, not where the cotton grew | No | Does the claim describe the cotton or the towel |

Two points follow from the table. A trademarked name, such as Egyptian Cotton or Supima, can be checked with the body that licenses it. A general name, such as "Pima" or "Indian cotton", cannot be checked that way, so it needs a written specification and, where the claim matters, evidence.

## Does the cotton claim cover the whole towel

A cotton claim usually applies to one part of the towel. Ask:

- **Pile or ground:** a towel has pile yarn, which forms the loops, and ground yarn, which forms the base cloth. A claim that covers only the pile is not a claim about the ground.
- **How much of it:** the trademarked names above are licensed for products made entirely from that cotton, so a blend of origins is not covered.
- **Which yarn:** extra-long staple fibre in a carded, open-end yarn gives a different towel from the same fibre combed and ring-spun.

## What counts as evidence

- The cotton variety and origin written into the specification, and into the quotation.
- For a licensed name, the licence number and the licensee's name, which you can confirm with the licensing body.
- A mill or supplier able to say where the cotton was bought, in a document that matches the towel's order.
- A test or inspection report where your product depends on the claim. Our guide to reading a towel test report explains what a report must show.
- A physical sample checked against the specification before the order.

A photograph of a label, or a claim on a website, is not evidence.

## What this guide does not cover

It does not say that one cotton is better than another for every towel, or give prices. It does not give the staple length of any cotton variety, which belongs in the supplier's own specification. It does not describe the cotton used in any towel we sell. And it does not cover labelling rules in your market, which set what a fibre-origin claim may say, so ask your customs broker or your trading-standards authority.

## What to ask a supplier

- Which cotton variety and which country, for the pile and for the ground?
- What is the staple length of the lot, and how is it measured?
- Is the name on the quote a trademark, and is the supplier licensed to use it?
- Can the claim be backed with a document, a licence or a test report?
- Is the claim the same on the quotation, the sample and the invoice?
