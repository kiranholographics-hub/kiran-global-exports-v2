A hotel does not replace towels on a calendar. It replaces them when they stop being fit to put in front of a guest: greyed, frayed at the hem, thinned in the pile, or simply gone. How quickly that happens depends far more on the property's own laundry and housekeeping than on any figure a supplier can quote. This guide sets out how buyers usually estimate their own replacement cycle, what shortens it, and why that estimate is worth writing into the specification before the first order.

It is general guidance. It does not give a lifespan for any towel we make: that depends on your laundry, and no honest supplier can promise it without knowing how the towels will be washed.

## Why the replacement cycle matters to a buyer

The replacement cycle decides two things a buyer cares about. The first is cost per use, which is the number that actually reaches the budget. A cheaper towel that has to be rebought sooner can cost more over a year than a dearer one that lasts. The second is reorder planning. A property that knows roughly when it will need the next batch can order it with enough lead time to match colour and construction to what is already in service, instead of buying in a hurry from whoever has stock.

## How properties usually estimate it

The usual approach works from wash life rather than months.

- Estimate how many commercial wash cycles a towel survives before it is no longer guest-presentable. Your laundry, or your linen supplier if you use one, will have a view from experience; this is the number that varies most.
- Estimate how many times each towel is washed in a year. That depends on occupancy, how often towels are changed, and how many sets you hold per room.
- Divide the first by the second. The result is a rough replacement interval for that towel in that property.

An example, with numbers chosen only to show the arithmetic: if a bath towel survives 200 washes and each towel is washed 100 times a year, it lasts about two years. Hold more sets per room, and each towel is washed less often, so the same towel lasts longer in calendar terms. That is why two hotels buying the identical towel can report very different lifespans.

## Par level and why it changes the answer

Par level is the number of complete sets of linen a property holds for each room: one in the room, one in the laundry, one on the shelf, and usually extra to cover loss and rejects. A higher par spreads the washing across more towels, which slows wear on each one and gives the laundry room to rest stock between cycles. A lower par means fewer towels doing more work. When you compare replacement cycles with another property, compare par levels too, or the comparison means little.

## What shortens a towel's life

Most of what wears a hotel towel out happens after it is delivered.

- Chemistry. Strong alkali and chlorine bleach lift stains but weaken cotton fibre, and overdosing does more damage than the dirt it removes.
- Heat. Drying hotter or longer than the load needs makes cotton brittle and harsh.
- Misuse. Towels used as cleaning cloths, or to remove make-up and fake tan, take stains that need harsher washing, and are often rejected early.
- Loss. Towels leave in guests' luggage and in pool bags. Loss is part of the replacement cycle even though nothing wore out.
- Mixed batches. Washing towels of different ages, colours or constructions together can transfer dye and shorten the life of the lighter ones.

None of this is a fault of the towel, and none of it is under the supplier's control. It is still worth knowing, because it is usually where a short replacement cycle comes from.

## What the specification can do

The towel's construction sets the ceiling on how long it can last; the laundry decides how close to that ceiling it gets. When you write a specification, these are the choices that bear on durability.

- Yarn. Ringspun cotton is the usual choice for towels that will be washed commercially at volume. Zero-twist is softer to the touch but suits gentler handling; our guide to towel constructions explains the trade-off.
- Weight. A heavier towel is not automatically a longer-lasting one, and it costs more to wash and dry. Our hotel towel GSM guide covers how to choose.
- Colour. White can be bleached back to white; coloured towels cannot, so colour needs a dyeing method chosen to hold through industrial washing. Our guide to lab dips explains how a colour is approved.
- Finishing. Hems, selvedges and the woven border are where a towel usually fails first. A border woven into the cloth will not peel or fade the way a printed one can.

## Why the cycle belongs in the brief

If you tell a supplier how the towels will be washed, how many sets you hold per room and roughly how long you need them to last, the supplier can propose a construction against that instead of guessing. It also gives both sides something to check a sample against. A buyer who sends only a weight and a size will get a towel that matches the weight and the size, and nothing more.

It also helps with the next order. A replacement cycle tells you when the reorder is due, and a reorder only matches what is in service if the specification, the approved sample and the colour reference are kept on file. Our guide to keeping a reorder consistent covers what to keep.
