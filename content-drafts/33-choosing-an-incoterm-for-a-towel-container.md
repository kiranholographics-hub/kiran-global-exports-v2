An Incoterm is a three-letter term in a sales contract that says who arranges and pays for each stage of the journey, and the point at which the risk of loss passes from seller to buyer. For a towel container shipped from India, the choice that matters most is how much of the journey you want the seller to handle. FOB leaves most of it with you. CIF adds the sea freight and insurance. DAP adds delivery to your door. The price changes with the term because the work behind it changes.

This guide compares the five terms importers most often meet on a towel order, shows where cost and risk pass under each, and ends with how to write the term into a contract.

It is general guidance. Incoterms are published by the International Chamber of Commerce, and the current edition is Incoterms 2020. The text of the rules governs; check it, or ask your forwarder, for anything specific to your shipment.

## What an Incoterm does, and what it does not

An Incoterm settles delivery, costs and risk between the two parties. It does not say when you pay, which is a payment-terms question, and our guide on payment terms covers that. It does not transfer ownership of the goods, and it does not settle what happens if the goods are defective. Those belong in the contract.

## Where cost and risk pass, term by term

| Term | Seller's job ends | Who pays the sea freight | Who insures | Who clears import |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| FCA | Goods handed to your carrier at a named place, export-cleared | You | You | You |
| FOB | Goods loaded on the ship at the named port, export-cleared | You | You | You |
| CFR | Goods loaded on the ship, with freight paid to your port | Seller | You | You |
| CIF | As CFR, with insurance bought for you | Seller | Seller buys the cover for your benefit | You |
| DAP | Goods ready to unload at your named place | Seller | No duty to insure; the seller bears the risk to that point | You |

Under FOB, CFR and CIF the risk passes to you once the goods are on board at the Indian port, even though under CFR and CIF the seller has paid for the voyage. That surprises many first-time buyers. A cheaper-looking CIF quote is not a quote where the seller is responsible for the ship.

## A worked scenario: the container is lost at sea

The easiest way to see the difference is to ask who loses if the container is lost after the ship has sailed.

- **FOB or CFR:** the loss is yours, because the risk passed when the goods were loaded. Under FOB it is only recoverable if you bought your own insurance.
- **CIF:** the loss is still yours, but the seller has bought insurance for you, so you claim against that. Under CIF the required cover is only the minimum level, known as Institute Cargo Clauses (C), which covers a narrow list of major events. Ask whether you want broader cover and who will pay for it.
- **DAP:** the loss is the seller's, because the risk stays with them until the goods reach your named place.

None of these is the right answer in general. A buyer who wants the seller to carry the voyage risk pays more for it, and is relying on the seller's own insurance and claims.

## A note on containers

FOB, CFR and CIF were written for goods loaded over the ship's rail. A towel container is not loaded that way: it is delivered to a terminal and handed to the carrier there. For that reason the Incoterms guidance points containerised cargo towards FCA instead, and it points the insurance-included versions towards CIP, which is the container counterpart of CIF.

In practice FOB is still widely used for container shipments, because it is familiar and quotations and letters of credit are written around it. You can usually keep using it. If you want the term to match how the container is actually handed over, ask for FCA and name the terminal. Incoterms 2020 also let the buyer and seller agree that the carrier issues an on-board bill of lading to the seller under FCA, which matters if the payment is by letter of credit.

## Which term suits which buyer

- **FOB or FCA:** you have a freight forwarder, want to choose the carrier and see the freight cost on its own line, and are happy to insure the cargo yourself. It is the usual choice for repeat importers.
- **CFR or CIF:** you want the seller to book the sea freight, but you accept the risk once the goods are loaded. Useful if you do not have a forwarder, as long as you read the freight and insurance lines and compare them with a quotation of your own.
- **DAP:** you want one price to your named place and the least handling. It costs more, because the seller carries the whole journey and prices the risk.

## What every term leaves with you

Under all five terms above the buyer handles the import side: customs clearance in your country, import duty and taxes, and delivery from the port where the term stops. A DDP quote, where the seller also takes on import clearance and duty, is the exception, and it needs a seller who can do that in your country. Duty rates depend on your country and are not covered here; our guide to HS codes for towels explains how the code is confirmed.

## How to write the term into the contract

- Name the term, the place and the edition together, for example "FOB Mumbai, Incoterms 2020". A term without a named place is incomplete.
- Say who pays the origin port and terminal charges. Some are included in a quotation and some are not.
- If you choose CIF, state the level of insurance you want, since the default is the minimum.
- Keep the term the same across the quotation, the proforma invoice and the contract.

## What this guide does not cover

It does not give freight or insurance prices, which depend on the route and date. It does not set out every Incoterm or every obligation under them, and it does not replace the ICC text. Which term suits your shipment also depends on your forwarder, your bank and your insurer.

## What to ask a supplier

- Which term is this quotation on, and at which named place?
- What does the price include at the origin port: loading, terminal charges, export clearance?
- If the term includes freight or insurance, what is the cover and who is the insurer?
- Can the quotation be restated on a different term so I can compare?
