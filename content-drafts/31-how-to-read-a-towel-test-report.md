A towel test report is worth what four details on it are worth: who tested, which sample, which method, and how the result compares with a limit you set. A report that gives a result without those four is a number, not evidence. And even a complete report describes the sample that went to the lab, not the shipment that will reach your warehouse, so it supports an approval and does not replace one.

This guide sets out what a report has to show, which tests are worth asking for on a terry towel, and how to read two common results against your own limits.

## What a test report can and cannot tell you

A report tells you how one specimen of one product behaved under a named test on a named date. That is useful, because it turns "good quality" into a figure you can hold the supplier to. It cannot tell you that the bulk order will match, that the colours you did not test will behave like the one you did, or that a supplier delivers on time. Our guides on reading a spec sheet and on reading a pre-shipment sample cover the rest of that approval.

## The five things every report must show

| What to look for | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| The laboratory, and whether it is accredited | An accredited lab has been assessed against ISO/IEC 17025 for a stated list of tests. Ask for the accreditation body's scope and check that your test is on it |
| What was tested | The report should identify the product, colour, construction and batch or sample reference, so you can tell it is your towel and not a similar one |
| The method, by name | A result is only comparable if the test is named, for example a dimensional change test under ISO 5077, not "shrinkage test" |
| The conditions | The washing procedure, number of cycles and temperature change the result, so they must be stated |
| The result, beside a requirement | A figure with no limit beside it cannot pass or fail. The limit should be the one in your specification |

On accreditation, the body that assesses labs in India is NABL, and its certificates refer readers to the scope of accreditation on its website. A lab can be accredited and still not hold the specific test you need in its scope, so check the scope, not only the certificate. Many national accreditation bodies take part in an international recognition arrangement, which is why an accredited lab's report is widely accepted across borders.

## Which tests to ask for on a terry towel

| Test | What it tells you | What to check in the report |
|---|---|---|
| Dimensional change after washing | How far the towel shrinks or grows. ISO 5077 measures it, using a washing and drying procedure from ISO 6330 | Percentage change in length and in width, how many cycles, which procedure. A minus sign is shrinkage |
| Colourfastness to washing | Whether colour is lost or other fabrics are stained in the wash. ISO 105-C06 | Grades for colour change and for staining, and the wash conditions |
| Colourfastness to rubbing | Whether colour transfers onto skin or cloth. ISO 105-X12 | Dry and wet grades separately |
| Colourfastness to light | How much the colour fades in light. ISO 105-B02 | The grade on the blue wool scale |
| Weight per unit area | Whether the towel is the GSM you specified | The figure, the method used, and your tolerance |
| Fibre composition | Whether the towel is the fibre you were quoted | The percentage and the method |
| Absorbency | How quickly and how much the towel takes up water | The method, because absorbency tests differ and results may not be comparable across them |

Our spec sheet guide explains the grade scales for the colourfastness tests. For towels sold in Europe, EN 14697 sets minimum requirements and test methods for new woven terry towels for contract and domestic use, and it draws on the same ISO 5077 and ISO 105 methods. This guide does not quote its limits, because they should be read from the standard itself and from the edition in force.

## Worked example: reading a shrinkage result

The figures here are hypothetical, chosen to show the arithmetic.

A report gives a dimensional change of −3.5 per cent in length and −2.0 per cent in width, after one wash cycle, for a towel specified at 70 × 140 cm. In a towel the warp usually runs along the length.

- Length: 140 × (1 − 0.035) = 135.1 cm
- Width: 70 × (1 − 0.020) = 68.6 cm

So the towel comes out of the wash at about 68.6 × 135.1 cm. Whether that is acceptable depends on the limit in your specification. If you set a limit of −4 per cent in length and −3 per cent in width, this result is within both. If you set −3 per cent in length, it is outside, even though the report itself says nothing wrong. A report records what happened. Your specification decides whether it passes.

## Worked example: reading a weight result

A towel specified at 500 GSM, with a tolerance of ±5 per cent that you agreed beforehand, has an acceptable range of 475 to 525 g/m².

- A report showing 520 g/m² is 4 per cent above the target, which is inside the range.
- A report showing 470 g/m² is 6 per cent below it, which is outside, and means less cotton than you specified.

The tolerance has to exist before the report arrives. Agreed afterwards, it is a negotiation, not a check.

## Traps worth looking for

- **A report for a different product.** Check the construction, weight and colour on the report against your sample, not just the supplier's name.
- **A report for a different colour.** Colourfastness depends on the dye as well as the cloth, so ask for the colours you are buying, including the darker ones, not only a white or a light shade.
- **An old report.** A report from before a new batch or a new dye lot says nothing about that batch. Check the date.
- **A pass with no figures.** "Pass" without a result and a limit cannot be checked. Ask for the full report.
- **Mixing a lab test with an inspection.** A lab test measures properties of a specimen. A pre-shipment inspection looks at the goods in the cartons. They answer different questions, and one does not stand in for the other.

## What this guide does not cover

It does not give pass or fail limits, which depend on your product, your customers and the standard you work to. It does not report any test results for our products, and it does not cover chemical safety rules in your country, which are separate and which your customs broker or a testing lab can advise on. The standards named here are revised from time to time, so confirm the edition a test specifies.

## What to ask a supplier

- Which laboratory tested this, and is the test within its accreditation scope?
- Which product, colour and batch does the report cover?
- Which method and conditions were used for each result?
- What limit is each result being compared with?
- Can I have the full report, with the figures, and the date it was issued?
