A pool towel gets a harder life than almost any other towel in a hotel, but the way it actually fails surprises most first-time buyers. It isn't usually the terry wearing thin. It's the towel looking visibly tired — bleached patches, dulled colour, faint yellow marks — while the fabric underneath is still perfectly functional. That gap between how it looks and how it performs is almost entirely a colour decision, not a fabric one.

## Why solid white is the wrong default

Chlorine bleaches unevenly rather than all at once, so a solid white pool towel develops patchy, faded areas that read as dirty even after a proper wash. Sunscreen and tanning oil leave marks that don't fully launder out of white cotton, and repeated sun exposure on loungers fades white faster than most buyers expect. None of this means the towel has failed structurally — it means it now looks like it has, which is what actually gets it pulled from circulation early.

Striped and patterned colourways hide all three problems. Chlorine patchiness, oil marks and sun fade are far less visible against a busy pattern or a mid-tone stripe than against a flat white background, which is why almost every resort and hotel pool programme that's been running for a while has moved away from solid white — the ones still using it are usually the newest programmes, not the most experienced ones.

## What the fabric still needs to get right

Colour choice solves the visible-wear problem, but the underlying fabric still has to do the actual job. Cotton terry remains the standard for the absorbency guests expect coming straight out of a pool — a lighter-weight or blended alternative dries faster on the shelf but noticeably underperforms wet against skin, which guests notice immediately at a pool in a way they might not elsewhere.

Wash durability matters more here than in a standard guest room, because pool towel turnover is higher — used once per lounger session rather than reused across a stay the way a bathroom towel often is. That means more laundry cycles per towel per week, which puts more pressure on pile retention than a typical in-room programme sees.

## Size and weight, for a different job than a bath towel

A pool towel is used lying on a lounger as much as it's used for drying, which changes the size logic compared to an in-room bath towel. Buyers tend to specify pool towels larger than an equivalent bath towel — enough to lie on comfortably, not just wrap around — while keeping the weight moderate rather than heavy, since a heavier towel takes longer to dry between uses in a high-turnover poolside laundry cycle and gets left out to dry between guest uses more often than an in-room towel would.

## Branding that survives sun, chlorine and repeated washing

Pool towels are one of the more commonly branded hospitality textiles — resort logos, property names — and the branding method needs to survive the same conditions the towel does. Woven labels and jacquard borders hold up best against chlorine and sun exposure over the towel's service life; heat-transfer branding is the first to show cracking or fading in this specific environment, faster than it would on an indoor bathroom towel that never sees direct sun.

## What to actually specify

Putting this together, a pool towel programme that holds up tends to share three decisions: a striped or patterned colourway rather than solid white, cotton terry construction at a weight chosen for reasonable dry time rather than maximum plushness, and a branding method matched to sun and chlorine exposure rather than picked for lowest upfront cost.
