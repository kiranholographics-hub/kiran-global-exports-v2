A towel minimum order quantity (MOQ) is not a number from a price list. It is the quantity at which a mill can cover the fixed cost of setting up your order and still sell at a price that makes sense. So it moves with what you ask for: a colour and construction the mill already runs sits at one end, and a new colour, a new design and your own branding sit at the other.

Figures quoted online vary enormously, from a few dozen pieces to several thousand, and most come from marketplace listings that do not say what the figure applies to. No single number is true for every supplier. This guide explains what sets a towel MOQ, so that you can ask a supplier the right questions and judge the answer.

## What sets a minimum

**Colour.** A new colour needs a dye recipe, a physical lab dip for you to approve and then a dye run. Dyeing is done in batches, and a batch has a practical size below which it is poor value to run. A colour the mill already dyes regularly carries less of that set-up. Our guide on lab dips explains the approval step, which is also where a custom colour starts costing time.

**Construction and design.** A woven design, such as a dobby border, is set at the loom. Preparing a loom for a new design takes time and yarn before the first sellable towel comes off it, and that cost is shared across however many towels follow. An existing construction avoids most of it. Our guide to towel constructions covers the options.

**Size and weight.** Each combination of size and GSM is a separate specification. Splitting an order across many of them can leave each below the level the mill treats as one run.

**Branding and packaging.** Woven labels, printed bands and printed packaging may carry their own minimums, separate from the towel. Our guide to briefing packaging for a private label order covers what to settle early.

**Shipping.** Pricing is usually built around filling a container, because freight per piece falls as the container fills. Our guide to container loading explains why the quantity that fits depends on carton specification and GSM, not on a fixed piece count.

## A worked example of why fixed costs matter

The figures here are hypothetical, chosen to show the arithmetic. Suppose setting up a custom colour and design costs 1,200 dollars in total, whatever the quantity.

| Order size | Set-up cost per piece |
|---|---|
| 1,000 pieces | 1.20 dollars |
| 5,000 pieces | 0.24 dollars |

The same set-up adds five times as much to each piece on the smaller order. That is why a supplier will quote a higher unit price for a smaller run, or decline it, and why the minimum is higher the more of the order is custom.

## Levers a buyer can ask about

These are questions to put to a supplier, not promises. Whether any of them is available depends on the mill and the product.

- **Use a colour and construction the mill already runs.** Ask what is available without a custom set-up, and what changes in the minimum if you choose it.
- **Ask whether quantities can be counted across colours or sizes.** Some mills count the total towards the minimum; others set it per colour or per size. Ask which applies.
- **Ask about a smaller run at a higher unit price.** This is the trade most often discussed: you pay more per piece for less commitment.
- **Start with an existing specification and move to custom later.** A first order on a standard specification can establish the relationship before you commit to a custom one.
- **Separate the towel from the branding.** Ask whether the towel minimum and the label or packaging minimum are the same figure or two.

## What a low MOQ can cost you

A low minimum is not automatically better. It usually comes with a higher price per piece, a narrower choice of colours and constructions, or both. A very low figure on a marketplace listing often applies to a stock item rather than to the custom specification you want, so check what the number refers to before relying on it. Whatever the quantity, the safeguards are the same: an approved physical sample before bulk production, and a lab-dip-approved colour.

## Which question to ask, by what you want

| If you want | What mainly drives the minimum | Ask |
|---|---|---|
| A plain towel in a standard specification | Container filling | What is the minimum for a standard range? |
| A custom colour | Dye batch and lab dip | What is the minimum per colour? |
| A custom design or border | Loom set-up | Is there a design charge or a higher minimum? |
| Your own labels and packaging | Label and packaging minimums | Are these separate from the towel minimum? |
| A mix of sizes and colours | Per-specification runs | Does the total count, or is it per line? |

## What this guide does not cover

It does not give the minimum for any product we make. That depends on the product, the level of customisation and the destination, and it is confirmed when you send your specification. It also does not cover the cost of samples or the freight terms, which are quoted separately.

## What to ask a supplier

- What is the minimum for this exact specification, and what does it count: pieces, colours, sizes or containers?
- What would change it: a different colour, a different construction, branding?
- Can quantities be combined across colours or sizes?
- Is there a smaller run available, and at what change in unit price?
- Are label and packaging minimums separate from the towel?
