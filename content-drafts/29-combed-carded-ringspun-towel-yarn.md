Two towels can have the same size, the same GSM and the same colour and still feel, wear and lint differently, because they are made from different yarn. The yarn is the line on a towel quotation that buyers read least and that explains most of the difference in price. This guide explains the four yarn terms that appear on towel quotations: carded, combed, ring-spun and open-end, plus ply and count. It ends with how to write them into your own specification.

It is general guidance. It does not describe the yarn in any towel we sell; that is confirmed on quotation, against your brief.

## First, where the yarn goes in a towel

A terry towel is woven from two sets of yarn. The **ground yarn** forms the flat base cloth that holds the towel together. The **pile yarn** forms the loops on both faces, the part that touches the skin and takes up water. Most of what a buyer feels is the pile, so most yarn questions are about the pile yarn. A quotation that names one yarn for the whole towel is leaving something out; ask for the pile and the ground separately.

## Carded or combed: how the fibre is cleaned

Raw cotton is opened and cleaned, then **carded**: passed between wire-covered rollers that untangle the fibres and lay them roughly parallel. Carded cotton still contains a share of short fibres and small impurities.

**Combed** cotton goes through an extra step. Fine combs pull out the shortest fibres and remaining impurities, leaving longer fibres lying more evenly. The result is a smoother, stronger, more even yarn that sheds less lint. The fibre removed is waste for that yarn, so combed yarn costs more than carded yarn from the same cotton.

What it changes in a towel:

- **Lint:** combed pile sheds less, which matters for a new towel's first washes and for black or dark colours where lint shows.
- **Feel and look:** combed loops are smoother and more even.
- **Strength:** longer fibres make a stronger yarn, so the loops pull out less easily.
- **Price:** carded is cheaper, and for a heavily laundered, cost-driven hotel or kitchen towel it can be the right choice.

## Ring-spun or open-end: how the yarn is spun

The fibre is then spun into yarn, mostly in one of two ways.

**Ring spinning** draws the fibres out and twists them continuously around a spindle. It makes a fine, strong, soft yarn with the fibres lying closely together. It is the slower and more expensive process.

**Open-end spinning**, also called rotor spinning, feeds fibre into a fast-spinning rotor that forms and twists the yarn. It is much faster and cheaper, and it handles shorter fibre, including some recycled cotton. The yarn is bulkier and more uneven, and usually feels harsher than ring-spun yarn of the same weight.

| Yarn | Feel | Strength and lint | Cost | Often used for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Combed ring-spun | Smoothest, softest | Strongest, least lint | Highest | Premium bath towels, spa and retail ranges |
| Carded ring-spun | Soft, a little less even | Good | Middle | Hotel and everyday towels |
| Open-end | Fuller but harsher | Weaker, more lint | Lowest | Budget, promotional and kitchen towels |

A "ringspun towel" on a product page usually means ring-spun pile yarn. It says nothing about whether the cotton was combed. If that matters to you, ask.

## Ply and twist

Yarn can be **single** (one strand) or **two-ply** (two strands twisted together). Two-ply pile is stronger and more durable and gives a fuller loop, at a higher cost and usually with a slightly firmer feel. Single-ply pile can feel softer and dries faster.

**Twist** matters too. A low-twist yarn feels softer and fluffier but wears faster; a higher twist is harder-wearing. **Zero twist** is a special case: the pile is spun with a dissolvable support yarn that is washed out after weaving, leaving loops of untwisted fibre that feel very soft. ## Count: how thick the yarn is

Yarn thickness is given as a **count**. Cotton is usually counted on the English system, written Ne or with an "s", as in 16s or 20s. A higher number means a finer yarn. Coarse counts are common for pile yarn because they make a bulky, absorbent loop; finer counts are common for the ground and for lightweight towels.

The count interacts with GSM. A heavy towel can be made with coarse yarn and fewer loops, or with finer yarn and more of them, and the two feel different at the same weight. That is one reason GSM alone does not describe a towel; our guide to reading a towel spec sheet goes through the other reasons.

## How to put yarn into your specification

You do not need to name every figure. You do need to say what matters to you, so the manufacturer cannot fill the gap with the cheapest option. For example:

- "Pile: 100% cotton, combed, ring-spun, two-ply. Ground: 100% cotton, ring-spun."
- "Pile: ring-spun cotton, carded acceptable. Lint after first wash to be checked on the sample."
- "Zero twist pile for the spa range; standard ring-spun pile for the guest-room range."

Then check it on the sample. Wash it the way it will be washed in use and look at lint, loop pull-out and hand feel against the brief. A yarn line on a quotation is a promise; the washed sample shows whether it was kept.

If you are comparing two quotes, put the yarn lines side by side before the prices. A combed ring-spun towel and an open-end towel at the same GSM are different products, and the price gap between them is the yarn.
