A terry towel is two fabrics woven together: a flat base cloth, and a layer of loops standing out of it. GSM weighs both at once, so two towels with the same GSM can carry quite different amounts of loop. This guide explains how the loops are formed, which features of the pile a buyer can ask about, and what each one tends to change. It is general guidance. It does not describe the pile of any towel we make, which is confirmed on quotation, against your brief.

## How the loops are made

Terry is woven with two sets of warp yarn. The ground warp is held under firm tension and forms the base cloth. The pile warp is run under lighter tension, so that it can be pushed into loops as the weft is beaten up. The height of the loops is set by the loose picks, two weft yarns that are inserted at a chosen distance from the edge of the cloth. A longer loose-pick distance gives taller loops.

In the most common pattern, the 3-pick terry, one loop is formed for every three weft yarns, so two picks sit under the loop and one sits between loops. Patterns that repeat the loop every four, five or more picks are less common, and the sources describe higher pick patterns as avoided mainly for cost. In a towel with loops on both faces, the loops alternate between the front and the back of the cloth from one group to the next.

## What a buyer can ask about

| Feature | What it is | What it tends to change | What to ask |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pile height | The length of the loops, set by the loose-pick distance | Longer loops hold more water, make a thicker towel and raise its weight. Short, dense loops give a thinner towel | Ask for a reference sample, since pile height is rarely given as a figure on a quotation |
| Loop density | How many loops there are in a given area | Denser loops raise the weight, and so the GSM | Ask whether the GSM was reached by taller loops, denser loops or a heavier base cloth |
| Pick pattern | How many weft yarns sit in each loop repeat, such as 3-pick or 4-pick | Changes how the loops are formed and spaced | Ask which pattern is used. Judge the result on a sample, not on the number |
| Loops on one or both faces | Terry with loops on both sides, or on one side only | Loops on both sides give the most absorbent cloth | Ask which faces carry loops, and read our guide on terry and woven towels |
| Cut or uncut loops | Velour has the loops sheared | Sources describe uncut loops as the most absorbent. The sheared face is smoother and takes embroidery well | Ask whether the loops are cut. Our guide to bathrobes covers velour |
| Yarn in the pile | The cotton and the twist of the pile yarn | A loosely twisted pile yarn is softer and more absorbent, a tightly twisted one rougher | Read our guide on constructions and yarn, and ask for the yarn count |

The table gives tendencies, not rules. Sources agree that a longer, denser pile means a heavier towel that holds more water and dries more slowly, and the right balance depends on how the towel is used and laundered.

## Why GSM alone does not tell you the pile

GSM is the weight of one square metre of the finished cloth. It includes the ground and the pile together. The figures below are hypothetical and show the arithmetic. They are not the weights of any towel.

| Towel | GSM | Hypothetical ground weight | Hypothetical pile weight |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 500 | 150 g/m² | 350 g/m² |
| B | 500 | 200 g/m² | 300 g/m² |

Both towels weigh 500 g/m², and each measures 70 × 140 cm, which is 0.98 m², so each weighs 0.98 × 500 = 490 g. Yet towel A carries 350 ÷ 300 = 1.17 times the pile of towel B, about 17 per cent more. Towel B spends part of its weight on a heavier base cloth. The two will feel and wear differently, though the specification line is identical.

## Checking the pile on a sample

- Look at both faces. Loops should stand evenly, with no bare line or patch. Our guide to towel defects explains missing pile and uneven loops.
- Weigh the sample and compare it with the specification, allowing for the tolerance you agreed.
- Wash it the way your laundry washes towels, and look again. Loops that flatten after washing show it quickly.
- Compare a sample against the approved sample in the same light, and on the same faces.

## What this guide does not cover

It does not give pile heights, loop densities or pick counts for any product, because the sources used here do not set them. It does not give a pile-to-ground ratio, and we found no reliable source for one, so it is not stated. Much of the weaving detail comes from technical descriptions and patents, so treat it as an outline of how terry is made, not as a loom specification. It also says nothing about how a particular mill sets its looms.

## What to ask a supplier

- Is this towel woven 3-pick or another pattern, and are the loops on both faces?
- Are the loops cut or uncut?
- What are the pile yarn and the ground yarn, and their counts?
- Was the GSM reached with taller loops, denser loops or a heavier ground?
- Can I have a sample, and can I wash it before I approve?
