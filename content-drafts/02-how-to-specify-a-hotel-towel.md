How to specify a hotel towel

--- BODY ---

A hotel towel does not wear out the way a domestic one does. It is washed at higher temperatures, with stronger chemistry, far more often, and it is judged by a guest who has no reason to be generous about it. Specify it like a domestic towel and it fails in one of three ways.

## The three ways it fails

The pile mats down. Loops that were soft on delivery flatten and stop standing up, so the towel feels thin and stops absorbing properly. This is usually a yarn question: combed ringspun cotton is twisted tighter than carded cotton and holds its pile through far more wash cycles.

The white goes grey. Repeated hot washing with bleach dulls a white towel that was not built for it. Nothing rescues a towel at this stage, which is why housekeeping ends up retiring a whole set early.

The colour drifts. The third order no longer quite matches the first two, so a linen room ends up with two shades of the same towel in circulation. This is a dyeing question, not a weaving one.

## What answers each

For the pile, ask about the yarn rather than the weight. Zero twist yarn gives the softest hand and high absorbency; ringspun gives the most durable pile through commercial laundering. Which one is right depends on whether your guests notice softness on the first night or consistency over a year.

For colour, ask whether the mill dyes by lab dip approval before bulk. A lab dip is a small dyed swatch you approve before the bulk dyeing runs. It is the difference between a reorder that matches and one that nearly does. Vat dyeing holds colour through industrial washing better than cheaper methods, which matters most for anything coloured that is washed daily.

For consistency across orders, ask what happens on the second shipment. A mill that checks each batch against the sample you originally approved will still match in two years. A mill that works from a fresh interpretation each time will not.

## The border is not decoration

Almost every hotel programme specifies a bordered towel, and the reason is practical rather than aesthetic. A woven dobby border is a reference line: housekeeping and guests both use it to tell a hand towel from a bath towel from a bath mat when several sizes are stacked together. Because it is woven in rather than printed, it does not fade out of step with the rest of the towel.

## Specify the set, not the towel

A property does not buy bath towels. It buys bath towels, bath sheets, hand and face towels, bath mats, pool towels and robes, and a guest sees them together. Specifying them as one matched set — same white, same border, same hand — is the difference between a bathroom that looks designed and one that looks assembled from whatever was in stock.

It also simplifies reordering: one approved standard across the property rather than six that drifted apart.

## Weight, honestly

Heavier is not better in hospitality. A heavier towel feels generous and absorbs more; it also takes longer through the dryer, costs more per wash and weighs more to ship. Properties running high occupancy and tight laundry turnaround often specify lighter than they first want to, and guests do not notice. Luxury properties with slower turnover go the other way.

Decide by laundry throughput and guest expectation, in that order, and ask the mill what weight delivers the feel you are describing.

## Before you commit

Get a physical sample, wash it the way your laundry will wash it, and look at it again. Not once — several times. A towel that still looks right after ten industrial cycles is telling you something a specification sheet cannot.
