By the time a pre-shipment sample reaches a buyer, the order has already been through development, an approved specification and an approved lab dip. It's tempting to glance at it, confirm it looks like what was agreed, and sign off. That glance misses most of what the sample is actually there to catch.

## What a pre-shipment sample actually is

It's a sample cut from the real bulk production run — not the original development sample made weeks or months earlier to establish the specification. The development sample proves the design and construction are right. The pre-shipment sample proves the actual bulk batch matches that approved specification, at the scale it was really produced at.

## Colour, checked against the right reference

Compare the pre-shipment sample against the approved lab dip swatch — the physical one, not the original photo or Pantone reference (see our guide on lab dip and colour matching for why that distinction matters). Bulk dyeing runs at a different scale than a lab dip, and a serious buyer checks that the batch stayed within a normal, reasonable tolerance of what was approved, not that it's identical to the eye in every lighting condition.

## Weight and hand-feel, not just GSM on paper

A spec sheet number and the way a towel actually feels in hand can diverge if the yarn or finishing process wasn't followed precisely. Handle the pre-shipment sample the way an end user would — check the weight feels consistent with what the original approved sample felt like, not just that a GSM figure is written down somewhere.

## Construction details that are easy to skim past

Check the hem and border stitching for consistency, not just that a hem exists. Check colourfastness by lightly rubbing a damp white cloth against a dyed section — colour transfer onto the cloth is a sign of incomplete dye fixing. Check any embroidery, jacquard border or logo placement matches position and alignment across multiple pieces in the sample, not just the first one you pick up.

## Size, checked with a tape measure

Confirm actual dimensions against the agreed specification rather than assuming a size is correct because the towel "looks about right" next to a reference. Small deviations compound at bulk scale in ways that are hard to see piece by piece.

## Packaging and labelling, if that was part of the order

If retail-ready packaging, private label tags or specific carton marking were agreed, the pre-shipment sample is the point to confirm they were executed as specified — not to discover a labelling error after the container has sailed.

## What a red flag actually looks like

A single small variation across a sample set is often just normal production tolerance. What's worth raising immediately is a pattern — colour consistently reading off across multiple pieces, a hem finish that's visibly different in quality, or a weight that feels noticeably lighter than the approved sample. That's the difference between something to note and something to hold the shipment on.

## Why this step exists at all

A pre-shipment sample is not a formality between approval and shipping — it's the last point where a mismatch can be corrected before a full container is packed and en route. Buyers who read it properly are checking the bulk run against what they approved, not just checking that a box arrived.
