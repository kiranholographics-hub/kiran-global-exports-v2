Buyers, mills and inspectors use the same words for towel defects and often mean different things by them. One person's "slub" is another's "yarn fault", and a shade difference that one list calls minor is major on the next. The words are easy to agree on. The harder part is agreeing how serious each defect is, and that has to be written down before the goods are packed, or the argument starts at the inspection.

This guide describes the defects you are most likely to meet on a finished terry towel, what causes them, and how buyers usually class them. It is general guidance, and the classes are examples to adapt, not a standard.

## The defects, in plain words

| Defect | What it is | Where it comes from | How it is often classed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Slub | A thick spot in the yarn, where a bunch of fibres has little or no twist and the yarn is wider than normal | Spinning. In terry, a slub or knot can drag pile yarn back from loops already made | A single small slub is often minor. Several in the same place, or one that disturbs the loops, is more serious |
| Missing pile | A line or a patch where the loops are missing, so the base cloth shows | Weaving. Missing pile yarns leave a line, and a pile-less spot is an area without loops | Often major where it shows in use, because it is visible and does not wash out |
| Uneven or ragged loops | Loops of different height, or mixed up, in a patch | Weaving, for example pile ends trapped against the reed wires | Depends on how large and how visible it is |
| Float or broken pick | A float is yarn that has failed to interlace over an area, and a broken pick is a gap in the weft caused by a break in the yarn | Weaving, in the ground cloth or the border | Usually major if it is in the body, and minor only if it is small and hidden |
| Hole | A break through the cloth | Weaving, finishing or handling | Almost always major |
| Oil or grease mark | A stain from machine oil or from contamination in transport | Weaving or finishing machinery, or transport | Depends on whether it washes out. A mark that stays is usually major |
| Shade variation | A difference in depth of colour within a piece, or from one piece or lot to the next | Dyeing and finishing | Varies most. Judge it against the approved sample and say how large a difference is allowed |
| Colour rubbing off | Colour transferring from the towel to another surface, which is called crocking | Dyeing. It is measured by a test, not by eye | A test result against a limit. See below |
| Hem or border faults | Skipped stitches, an uneven or crooked hem, loose threads, a border out of line | Sewing and cutting | Often minor if small, and major if the hem is open or the towel is visibly crooked |
| Size or weight outside tolerance | The towel is not the size or weight in the specification | Weaving and finishing | Major once it is outside the tolerance you agreed |

## Why classes differ between lists

A defect list ranks problems by how they affect the sale of the towel and its working life. Minor defects are faults that would not influence a purchase, and major defects are those that are likely to, and such goods are often sold as seconds. The same sources do not agree on every item. One garment list puts minor shading variation between pieces under minor defects, and another puts it under major. So the class belongs in your own defect list, not in an assumption that the inspector shares your view.

Fabric is sometimes inspected by a four-point system, in which each defect scores points by its length. That suits rolls of cloth. For finished towels, the count of defective pieces in a sample is more usual, and our guide to pre-shipment inspection explains how that count works.

## Writing your own list: an example

The thresholds below are examples to show the form. They are not a standard, and you should set your own.

| Defect | Example rule | Class |
|---|---|---|
| Slub | One small slub on one face | Minor |
| Slub | A slub that has pulled loops | Major |
| Missing pile | Any visible line or patch | Major |
| Hole | Any | Major |
| Oil mark | Remains after one wash | Major |
| Shade variation | Visible against the approved sample at normal viewing distance | Major |
| Loose thread | One thread, easily trimmed, with no open seam | Minor |

A good rule says what is seen, where, and in what condition: after a wash, at what distance, against which reference. A rule that says only "visible" will be read differently by every inspector.

## Defects you cannot see

Two problems are found by measurement and not by eye. Colour that rubs off is measured by a rubbing test, and a towel that passes in dry rubbing can still transfer colour when wet, so ask for both. Our guide to reading a towel test report explains how colourfastness is reported. Size and weight need a tape and a scale, used the way the specification says. Our guide to reading a towel spec sheet covers how those lines should be written.

## When you find a defect

- Photograph it with a ruler beside it, and keep the piece.
- Note the carton and the position in the shipment, because the pattern of defects matters as much as the count.
- Compare it against your defect list, not against memory.
- Raise it in writing, with the photographs. Our guide to receiving a container explains the notice periods and who to tell.

## What this guide does not cover

It does not set acceptance limits for your product, or say how many defects a lot may contain. That is the AQL you agree, and our inspection guide covers it. The class of each defect is an example, not a rule, and the sources it draws on disagree in places. The standard ASTM D3990 gives defined terms for fabric defects, including the broken pick and colour transfer by rubbing; check its exact wording if you intend to write it into a contract.

## What to ask a supplier

- Which defect list do you inspect against, and can I see it?
- How do you class shade variation, and against what reference?
- What do you do with pieces that have a defect, and are seconds packed with first-quality goods?
- Can the defect list for my order be agreed before production?
