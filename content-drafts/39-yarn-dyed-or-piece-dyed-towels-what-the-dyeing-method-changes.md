Two separate decisions sit behind the colour of a towel. The first is when the colour goes on: into the yarn before weaving, which is yarn-dyeing, or onto the finished cloth, which is piece-dyeing. The second is which dye is used. A quotation that says only "dyed" answers neither, and the two choices change what the towel can look like, how a colour is matched and approved, and how well it holds up in the wash.

This guide explains both choices, which suits stripes, borders and solid colours, and what to check on a sample. It is general guidance. It gives no prices, and it describes our own towels only where the catalogue already does.

## When the colour goes on

| Method | What it is | What it suits | What to weigh |
|---|---|---|---|
| Yarn-dyed | The yarn is dyed before it is woven, so the cloth is built from coloured yarn | Stripes, coloured borders and multicolour woven designs, because the pattern is made by the yarns themselves | Usually costs more, and the pattern is fixed when the loom is set up, so it suits a design you intend to keep. Each coloured yarn has to be matched |
| Piece-dyed | The cloth is woven first, then dyed as a whole piece | Solid colours, and large volumes. A vendor guide calls it the most cost-effective choice for solid colours | A single colour across the towel. The same woven cloth can be dyed in different colours, but a pattern in several colours generally needs yarn-dyeing |

For terry towels, sources agree on the main point: stripes and jacquard designs come from yarn that was coloured before weaving, and plain solid-colour towels are typically piece-dyed. Borders are a separate question, since a coloured border on a white towel is made by weaving coloured yarn into that part of the cloth, which is a yarn-dyed element on an otherwise plain towel. Our guide on towel constructions covers dobby and jacquard borders.

## Which dye is used

Reactive dyes are the dominant class for cotton. A reactive dye forms a chemical bond, called a covalent bond, with the cellulose of the cotton. A pigment is not bonded to the fibre. It is held on the surface by a binder. Our plain-dyed towels are piece-dyed with reactive dyes, with Pantone matching available, as our catalogue states.

The effect on colour holding is generally in favour of reactive dyes for washing and rubbing, but the studies are not uniform. One comparison on printed cotton found pigment inferior on rubbing fastness, yet comparable on colour fastness overall, and better on some comfort properties. So do not choose on the name alone. Ask for the colourfastness results for the colour you are buying, and our guide to reading a towel test report explains the washing, rubbing and light tests.

## Choosing by product

| If you are buying | The usual route | Check |
|---|---|---|
| Solid-colour bath towels in volume | Piece-dyed | Colour approved by a lab dip before bulk dyeing, and a written colour reference. Our lab dip guide explains why |
| A white towel with a coloured stripe or border | The stripe or border is made from coloured yarn | Which yarns are coloured, and whether the colour runs onto the white after washing |
| A multicolour woven design | Yarn-dyed | The number of colours, the design set-up and whether it will be kept for reorders |
| A coloured range for a hotel or a brand | Piece-dyed for solids, yarn-dyed for patterns | Colourfastness to washing and rubbing for the darkest colour |

## Checking the sample

- Look at the colour in the same light as your approved reference, on both faces.
- Wash the sample the way your laundry washes towels, and look again. Dark and strong colours deserve the most attention.
- For a stripe or border beside white, wash it with white items and check whether any colour has moved.
- Ask for the rubbing results, wet and dry, separately.
- Keep the sample and the report together with the order.

## What this guide does not cover

It does not give prices, dye recipes or minimum quantities, which depend on the colour and the design and are confirmed on quotation. It does not say that one method is better than another in every case. Much of the comparison between yarn-dyed and piece-dyed comes from vendor guides and trade pages, not from a technical standard, so treat the cost points as general. It does not rank dyes beyond the reactive and pigment contrast described above, and its statement about reactive dyes applies to cotton.

## What to ask a supplier

- Is the colour yarn-dyed or piece-dyed, and for a pattern, which yarns are coloured?
- Which class of dye is used, reactive or pigment?
- Can I have the colourfastness results for my colour, not only for a white or light shade?
- How is the colour matched and approved before bulk, and what is kept on file for a reorder?
- Can I have a sample to wash before I approve?
