### Article A: How a terry towel is made, from yarn to finished towel

*About 850 words. For Textile Learner or Textile Fashion Study.*

A terry towel looks simple: a square of cotton with loops on both sides.
Making one takes two sets of warp yarn, a loom that can weave loops on
purpose, and several finishing steps that decide how the towel will feel
after its fiftieth wash. This article follows a cotton terry towel
through the main stages of production, as they are carried out in
towel-weaving centres such as Solapur in Maharashtra.

**1. Yarn: two warps and a weft**

Three groups of yarn go into a terry towel. Ground warp and weft are
woven together into the plain foundation fabric that gives the towel its
strength. Pile warp is woven over that foundation as loops, on one face
or both, and it is the pile that dries the user.

The pile yarn is usually the softer, bulkier yarn, often ring-spun
cotton, sometimes combed or low-twist for a softer hand. The ground yarn
needs strength more than softness, because it carries the tension of
weaving and holds the loops in place through years of laundering.

**2. Warping and sizing**

Before weaving, the warp yarns are wound in parallel onto beams. A terry
loom needs two: one for the ground warp and one for the pile warp. They
are kept separate because they are fed into the loom at different
tensions, which is the key to forming loops.

The ground warp is usually sized: coated with a starch-based or
synthetic size that protects it from abrasion on the loom. Size is
washed out later, during processing.

**3. Weaving the loops**

The loops are formed by a weave usually called three-pick terry. The
loom inserts weft yarns in groups of three. For the first two picks the
reed beats them only part of the way into the cloth. On the third pick
the reed beats all three into place together. Because the pile warp is
fed at a low tension, the movement of the three picks pushes the pile
yarn up into loops on the face of the cloth, while the ground warp,
held tight, stays flat.

Loop height depends on how far the first two picks are left short of the
cloth, and on how much pile yarn is let off. Taller loops make a thicker,
heavier towel; shorter loops make a flatter towel that dries faster.
Borders, plain bands and woven patterns such as dobby borders are made
at the same time by changing the weave in those areas.

**4. Processing: scouring, bleaching and dyeing**

The woven cloth, called grey or greige towelling, is still stiff and
contains size, natural waxes and impurities from the cotton. Scouring
removes these so the cotton can absorb water and take dye evenly. White
towels are bleached. Coloured towels are dyed either as yarn, before
weaving, or as finished cloth, after weaving.

Yarn-dyed towels allow stripes, checks and jacquard patterns in several
colours. Piece-dyed towels are one colour all over and are common for
plain hotel and retail ranges. Either way, the colour is usually
approved against a small dyed sample, a lab dip, before bulk dyeing.

**5. Finishing**

Finishing gives the towel its final feel. A softener is applied to the
cloth, then it is dried, often in a tumble dryer, which opens up the
loops and makes the pile fuller. Some towels are sheared on one side to
make velour, a smooth face with cut loops.

**6. Cutting, hemming and labelling**

Towels are usually woven as a long continuous strip, with plain bands
marking where one towel ends and the next begins. The strip is cut at
those bands, and the cut ends are hemmed. The long edges, called
selvedges, are finished on the loom. Labels with fibre content, care
instructions and the brand are sewn in at this stage.

**7. Inspection and packing**

Each towel is checked for weaving faults, such as missing loops, broken
pile or uneven borders, and for stains, shade differences and measuring
errors. Weight is checked against the specification: a towel's weight
per square metre (GSM) is the figure buyers most often specify, and it
moves with loop height, yarn count and finishing. Finished towels are
folded, packed and cartoned to the buyer's packing instructions.

**Why the process matters to a buyer**

Most of what a buyer notices in a towel is decided during production,
not on the specification sheet. Softness comes from the pile yarn and the
finishing; absorbency from good scouring and the loop structure;
durability from the ground yarn and the weave; colour consistency from
lab dip approval and dyeing control. A buyer who understands the stages
can ask the right questions: what yarn is in the pile and the ground,
how is the colour approved, and how is the finished towel checked
against the approved sample.

### Article B: Five mistakes buyers make when ordering hotel towels from overseas

*About 750 words. For Texfash or HospitalityNet.*

Hotels and hospitality suppliers increasingly buy towels directly from
manufacturers abroad, India among them, rather than through local
wholesalers. The savings can be real. So are the problems, and most of
them start before production, in what the buyer asks for. These are the
five mistakes we see most often from the manufacturing side, and how to
avoid each one.

**1. Specifying weight and nothing else**

"600 GSM bath towel" is not a specification. GSM, the weight of one
square metre of towel, says nothing about the size of the towel, the
yarn in the loops, the loop height, the border or the finish. Two 600
GSM towels can feel and perform completely differently. A usable
specification states the finished size, the GSM with a tolerance, the
yarn for the pile and the ground, the border style, the colour, and the
labels and packing.

**2. Approving a sample without washing it**

A towel straight from the mill has been softened and tumble dried; it
has not yet met a commercial laundry. Cotton shrinks in its first hot
washes, and a sample that measures correctly on delivery can lose
several centimetres. Wash the sample the way your laundry will, then
measure and weigh it. If it no longer meets the specification, the time
to change it is before bulk production, not after the first container.

**3. Leaving colour to a description**

"Ivory", "sand" and "hotel white" mean different things to different
mills. Colour should be approved physically, with a lab dip: a small
piece of cotton dyed to the target shade and checked under agreed
lighting before any bulk dyeing. Keep the approved lab dip. It is the
reference for every reorder, and the only way to make next year's towels
match the ones already in your linen room.

**4. Treating the first order as a one-off**

Hotel towels are rebought constantly as towels are lost, stained and worn
out. A towel that cannot be reordered to match is a problem for years.
Ask the manufacturer what they keep on file: the approved sample, the
lab dip, the yarn and weave details. Keep your own copy of the same
record. A specification that lives only in an email thread will not
survive a change of staff on either side.

**5. Forgetting the paperwork until the goods ship**

An overseas order comes with documents: a commercial invoice, a packing
list, a bill of lading and usually a certificate of origin. Your customs
broker will also need the right tariff classification and any labelling
your market requires, such as fibre content and country of origin.
Settle who prepares what, and what the labels must say, at the quotation
stage. Late changes to labels or packing are the most avoidable cause of
delay in an otherwise good order.

**What good looks like**

None of this needs a large purchasing department. It needs a
specification written down, a sample that is washed and approved, a
colour fixed by lab dip, a record kept for reorders, and the paperwork
agreed early. Manufacturers that work this way will welcome the
questions; a supplier who resists them is telling you something before
the first order is placed.
